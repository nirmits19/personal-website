Perfection is the killer of speed, unless it's the 911

Our whole lives, we have been sold the same idea. That perfection is what is needed. That it is what is expected of us, at every moment, in every decision, in every output. That if we are not perfect, we will not succeed and we will not survive.

But the truth is, perfection in this day and age is hard to come by. And when you do manage to reach for it, it almost always comes at the cost of the one thing that matters more: speed.

The Cost of Waiting for Perfect

Perfection sounds noble. It feels responsible. It looks like care. But underneath, it is often something else entirely. It is fear dressed up as discipline. It is procrastination wearing a suit.

When you are chasing perfect, you are not actually moving. You are circling. Refining. Reconsidering. You tell yourself you are getting closer, but the goalpost keeps shifting because perfect is not a real destination. It is a horizon. It moves with you.

Meanwhile, the world keeps going. Other people are shipping. Other people are deciding. Other people are getting things wrong, fixing them, and getting them right the second time. By the time the perfectionist looks up, the race is already over.

Europe vs the US and China

You can see this play out at the scale of entire continents.

Look at Europe. The regulations, the consultations, the rounds of review. The EU spends so much time trying to perfect the best outcome that it sits around waiting for the perfect moment to act. By the time a decision is made, the conditions that prompted it have already changed.

Compare that to the US and China. Less regulation. More agency. The willingness to make decisions before the picture is complete, knowing that imperfect action beats perfect inaction every time. They are not always right. They are sometimes deeply wrong. But they are moving. And in a world that rewards motion, moving is most of the battle.

This is not a political point. It is a philosophical one. Cultures, like people, can become so afraid of getting it wrong that they forget how to act at all. The price of caution is often invisible until it is too late to pay it back.

The Exception: The 911

The only real exception is the Porsche 911.

It is arguably the best, and the most beautifully boring, supercar you can buy. Decades of research. Generations of refinement. Every iteration a little sharper than the last. The 911 has become the standard against which every other supercar is measured.

But here is the thing. Porsche did not get there by sitting around waiting for the perfect design. They got there by building, releasing, learning, and rebuilding. Every 911 was an imperfect step toward the next one. Perfection was not the starting point. It was the slow accumulation of a thousand decisions made before they were ready.

That is the real lesson hiding inside the 911. Perfection, when it does happen, is not a moment. It is a byproduct of years of imperfect motion.

Speed is the Real Currency

You will be more respected if you reach sixty in 2.1 seconds than if you reach sixty in fifteen seconds because you abided by every rule along the way.

The world does not remember the people who waited for the perfect conditions. It remembers the ones who moved. The ones who shipped something rough and made it better in public. The ones who were willing to be wrong out loud so they could eventually be right in a way that mattered.

Speed is the real currency of progress. Perfection is what you tell yourself you are chasing when you are too scared to start.

Think Less. Act More.

The shift is simple, even if it is not easy. Stop trying to get it right the first time. Start trying to get it out the first time. Let the world tell you what is wrong with it. Let reality be your editor instead of your imagination.

The people building the future are not smarter than you. They are just less afraid of looking stupid in the meantime. They have made peace with the fact that mistakes are not the opposite of progress. They are the raw material of it.

Think less. Act more.

Maybe one day, you will be able to afford the 911.
